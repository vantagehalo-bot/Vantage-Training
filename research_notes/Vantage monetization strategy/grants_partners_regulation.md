# Non-dilutive Funding, Partnership Channels and Regulatory/Liability Picture for Vantage (AI navigation for blind and low-vision users)

Research date: 2026-11-16. Scope: US, UK/EU, India, global. Tool budget was limited (~15 calls), so several named programs could not be verified this pass and are listed under Gaps rather than guessed.

## 1. Grants, prizes, accelerators and credits (amounts, eligibility, cycles, equity)

### Takeaway
The fastest, lowest-friction money for a student team is: (a) cloud credits (days, no equity), (b) prize/challenge cash such as MIT Solve ($10k guaranteed per selected team; 2027 call closed 2 Nov 2026) and the Hult Prize ($1M seed for one team), (c) in India, the Assistech Foundation accelerator (no equity) plus incubator-routed grants (NIDHI-PRAYAS up to INR 20 lakh, TIDE 2.0 up to ~INR 7 lakh, BIRAC BIG up to INR 50 lakh, Startup India Seed Fund up to INR 20 lakh grant), and (d) in the US, NSF I-Corps ($50k, students can lead) as the on-ramp to NSF SBIR Phase I (now up to $305k after the 2026 reauthorization). UK Smart Grants are closed/paused, and Microsoft AI for Accessibility's current status could not be confirmed from an official page.

## 3. Running a credible user pilot (ethics, panels, communities)

### Takeaway
Fable is the established paid panel of disabled testers, but it targets digital products (web/app), so it covers the companion app, not the wearable. For the physical device, a university IRB or ethics committee plus COMS-supervised field trials is the credible route. Only limited sourcing was gathered.

### Cited Findings
- Fable provides digital accessibility testing and research "powered by people with disabilities" — [Fable](https://makeitfable.com/); it is a platform for remote, on-demand testing of websites and digital products with users with disabilities — [Insight Platforms](https://www.insightplatforms.com/platforms/fable/)
- Developers on Reddit question whether Fable has non-business pricing, which suggests cost can be a barrier for students — [Reddit r/accessibility](https://www.reddit.com/r/accessibility/comments/1lcm0hs/disabled_usability_testing_where_to_find_users/)
- Published ETA evaluations use structured wayfinding tasks comparing devices such as WeWALK and UltraCane — [PMC ETA efficacy study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12909938/)

### Inferences
- A student team should run the pilot through its university's IRB (human-subjects research involving a vulnerable population plus physical-safety risk). Have a sighted spotter or COMS present for outdoor trials, start in controlled indoor courses, and record detection metrics (true positives, misses, false alarms per obstacle class) alongside user-reported workload/trust.

### Gaps
- IRB requirements, Fable pricing, and blind-community recruitment routes (NFB/ACB chapters, AppleVis, Be My Eyes community) were not sourced this pass.

## 4. Regulation: is an obstacle-detection mobility aid a medical device? (US, EU, UK, India) plus CE/FCC, liability and insurance

### Takeaway
In the US, manual mobility aids like canes are Class I, 510(k)-exempt and GMP-exempt (21 CFR 890.3075). FDA has no product code for "blind," "obstacle" or electronic travel aids in its classification database, so a powered AI obstacle detector does not map cleanly to an existing code. The team needs to choose its claims carefully and possibly ask FDA directly (513(g) request). In the EU, the MDR definition of a medical device includes "compensation for an injury or disability," so a mobility aid for blind people is very likely a medical device, probably Class I (self-certified). India regulates all medical devices under MDR 2017 (licensing for all classes since Oct 2023). Basic product liability insurance for small businesses is often quoted at $500-$3,000/year for $1M cover, but a safety-relevant navigation device will likely cost more.

### Cited Findings
- FDA "Cane" (product code IPS) and "Cane, Safety Walk" (KHY): Class 1, regulation 21 CFR 890.3075, 510(k) exempt, GMP exempt, Physical Medicine panel — [openFDA classification](https://api.fda.gov/device/classification.json?search=device_name%3A%22cane%22&limit=20)
- openFDA classification searches for device names containing "blind" and "obstacle" returned zero product codes — [openFDA "blind"](https://api.fda.gov/device/classification.json?search=device_name%3A%22blind%22&skip=0&limit=20); [openFDA "obstacle"](https://api.fda.gov/device/classification.json?search=device_name%3A%22obstacle%22&skip=0&limit=20)
- 21 CFR 890.5050 (Daily activity assist device) is 510(k)-exempt, subject to §890.9 limits, and exempt from QMS requirements except records/complaint files — [eCFR 890.5050](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-H/part-890/subpart-F/section-890.5050)
- EU MDR definition of a medical device covers purposes including "diagnosis, monitoring, treatment, alleviation of, or compensation for, an injury or disability" — quoted in [ScienceDirect review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S092188902600271X), which also notes the MDR covers devices helping persons with disability (contrasting ISO 13482 personal-care robots)
- India: since 1 Oct 2023, Class C and D devices that were previously non-notified also need CDSCO licences (Class A/B licensing came earlier) — [CliniExperts](https://cliniexperts.com/legal-implications-of-the-cdsco-license-update-for-class-c-and-class-d-medical-devices/); [iMarc Engineering](https://www.imarcengineering.com/news/medical-devices-rules-amendments-licensing-compliance). CDSCO's Class A non-sterile/non-measuring list includes mobility items such as "Parapodium walking frame" — [Seyecs Class A list](https://blogs.seyecs.com/cdsco-registration-for-class-a-medical-devices/)
- Product liability insurance: 63% of small businesses pay $60/month or less — [TechInsurance](https://www.techinsurance.com/product-liability-insurance/cost); $500-$3,000/yr for basic cover with typically $1M limits — [Routeway360](https://routeway360.com/how-much-is-product-liability-insurance/); hardware premiums "vary widely" by operations, stage, coverage and claims — [Vouch](https://www.vouch.us/blog/how-much-does-business-insurance-cost-for-hardware-companies)
- FCC: the FCC equipment authorization database lists accredited test labs and TCBs for radio devices (relevant if the wearable has Wi-Fi/BLE) — referenced via FCC equipment authorization tools (apps.fcc.gov); not separately verified in this pass

### Inferences
- US strategy options: (1) label it as a supplement to the long cane, with intended use "to provide environmental awareness information to users who are blind or have low vision," and get a regulatory opinion. The closest analog (cane, 890.3075) is Class I exempt, which suggests that even if FDA treats it as a device, the likely burden is registration/listing and complaint files rather than a 510(k). (2) Avoid disease/diagnosis claims. Whether FDA's general wellness policy applies is doubtful, because the device compensates for a disability. Consider a 513(g) request for a formal answer. The proposed "21 CFR 890.3925" is the wheelchair regulation, not a walking/blind aid (from prior knowledge; not verified in this pass).
- EU: under MDR, a non-invasive active device that isn't diagnostic would most likely be Class I via Annex VIII Rule 13. Software that only drives the device inherits its class, but standalone app software (Rule 11) may go up to IIa if it "provides information used to take decisions." The exact rule text could not be fetched (EUR-Lex scrape failed), so this needs verification. Class I means self-declared CE marking plus technical documentation, QMS, post-market surveillance and a PRRC.
- UK: MHRA follows the UK MDR 2002 (based on the old EU MDD); Class I devices are self-declared under UKCA and registered with the MHRA, and CE marks are still accepted under extended transitional arrangements (not verified this pass).
- India: the device would likely be a Class A/B medical device under MDR 2017 if it claims disability compensation. Registration/licensing goes through CDSCO's SUGAM portal and state licensing authorities (not verified).
- Liability: the key risks are a missed obstacle (false negative) leading to a fall or traffic injury. Mitigations: clear "supplementary aid, do not replace cane/guide dog" labeling in accessible formats, mandatory onboarding/training (ideally COMS-delivered), logging of detection confidence and fail-safe alerts (e.g. a distinct haptic signal when sensors fail or confidence is low), conservative marketing claims, and product liability + clinical trials insurance for pilots. Budget well above the basic $500-$3k/yr quotes because insurers price medical/safety devices higher (unquantified).

## 5. Privacy: on-device camera processing, GDPR and India DPDP

### Takeaway
The EDPB's late-2026 statement on smart glasses is directly relevant. A wearer's purely personal use may fall under GDPR's household exemption, but manufacturers and service providers still have obligations. EDPB expects visible recording indicators and notes that bystander consent is hard to get, which favors processing frames on the device without storing them. India's DPDP Rules were notified on 14 Nov 2025 with phased compliance.

### Cited Findings
- EDPB statement on smart glasses: personal use (e.g. sharing with family or personal translation in a private context) can fall under the household exemption; recording should be obvious through LEDs, audio signals and similar cues; "Even in public spaces, bystanders should clearly understand that someone is filming or photographing them" — [EDPB](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/news/edpb-publishes-statement-on-the-data-protection-challenges-of-smart-glasses_en)
- EDPB recommends recording indicators that operate by default, are hard to disable and are easy to notice; manufacturers will likely find valid bystander consent hard to obtain; the household exemption is narrowly construed (not for public sharing or professional use). Statement covered 28 Oct 2026 — [A&O Shearman](https://www.aoshearman.com/en/insights/ao-shearman-on-data/edpb-releases-smart-glasses-statement)
- EDPB Guidelines 3/2019 on video devices address the household exemption scope — [EDPB Guidelines 3/2019](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/sites/default/files/files/file1/edpb_guidelines_201903_video_devices_en_0.pdf)
- India DPDP Rules, 2025 were notified on 14 Nov 2025 after consultations — [PIB](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc20251117695301.pdf); the DPDP Act does not apply to personal data an individual has voluntarily made public — [Fisher Phillips](https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/insights/insights/indias-new-data-privacy-rules-are-here)

### Inferences
- Design for "no personal data leaves the device": run inference on the Pi, don't store or upload frames by default, blur faces and plates in any data-collection mode, and require explicit opt-in plus anonymization for training-data capture during pilots. A tension to resolve: a visible recording LED (EDPB expectation) may conflict with blind users' wish for discretion. Consider documenting that frames are processed transiently and not recorded, and run a DPIA.
- Collecting pilot training data (video of public streets) makes Vantage a controller under GDPR/DPDP, so the household exemption won't cover it.

### Gaps
